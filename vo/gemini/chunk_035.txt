What you never learn is how long the custom outlived you. In 1857, Parliament opened a divorce court. It was cheaper than an Act of Parliament, which isn't the same as cheap, and it wasn't fair. A husband needed to prove one thing. A wife needed to prove more. Wife sales faded, but they didn't vanish. As late as 1913, a woman told a court that her husband had sold her to a workmate for a pound. The custom lived on in the imagination, too. In 1886, Thomas Hardy opened a novel, The Mayor of Casterbridge, with a drunk man selling his wife at a country fair for five guineas. Readers recognised it at once. Hardy didn't have to invent a thing.
