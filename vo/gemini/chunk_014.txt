NARRATOR: You're twenty-nine when things start to fall apart. Ned argues with the landlord over a bill and is dismissed from the Hen and Chickens. It was the landlord's fault, he tells you. For some reason, it's always somebody else's fault. You move to one room. Ned finds evening work at a tavern, serving drinks. Mostly he drinks them. Instead of complaining, you take in washing. You stand over copper tubs from dawn, and your hands go red and then grey. You earn about six shillings a week, and on Friday night Ned collects it. After all, it's his. Every few weeks he finds the tin. To cover the rent, you take in a lodger. He's a brass-caster in his forties, a widower, who speaks about eleven words a week, four of them "thank you" . He mends your son's boots without being asked. He fixes the broken stair. He is, you notice, the only man in the house who fixes things. What you don't know is that Ned has found someone else. The barmaid at his tavern is expecting his child. The whole street knows. Nobody tells you. Nobody ever tells the wife. The truth is, Ned has a problem he can't solve. He can't afford a lawyer. He can't afford Parliament. He can't afford two households. But he remembers Peg, and a rope of straw.
