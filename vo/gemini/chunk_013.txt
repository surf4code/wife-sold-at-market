For a few years, it's genuinely good. You rent a room above a chandler's shop. On Saturdays Ned takes you to the market and buys you a ha'penny gingerbread, and you eat it slowly so that it lasts the whole walk home. In 1822, you have a daughter. In 1825, a son. Ned sings to both of them in the same cheerful voice he uses on the horses. In the quiet of the night you think, this is it. This is what the rules were for. Married life, you discover, runs on a timetable. You're up at five to fetch water from the pump and light the fire. You make Ned's breakfast, then the children's, then you scrub whatever the day has made dirty, which is everything. In the evening you mend, and cook, and listen to him describe his day. Nobody calls any of this work. They call it being a wife. Still, you keep a tin under the floorboard, with a few coppers in it. You aren't sure why. Peg's voice, perhaps. Legally the tin is his too. But it makes you feel better.
